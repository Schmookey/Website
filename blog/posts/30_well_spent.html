    Why second-hand doesn't mean second best.

    BLOG ENTRY

    £30 Well Spent

    UPDATED Wed 25 Nov 2026 19:00
    2026-11-25 19:00 GMT
    ⏱ – min read 💬 Replies: 0 ❤️ 0 🔁 0

    Around four years ago, I started having a niggling feeling that I should read more, or certainly I should read with more intention. I like reading and I like listening to audiobooks; I just felt I wasn't doing it enough. I kept the idea in my head and bought an Amazon Fire tablet. It was an impulse buy more than anything, as I didn't have a tablet up to that point. I signed up for Kindle and I bought The Sprawl Trilogy (again!), as well as a book titled Kicking Off in North Korea, a book about football and friendship in foreign lands. I ate those up, but I soon stopped picking up the tablet to read books with, as it felt cumbersome and a bit awkward holding a 10.1-inch device to read on. I decided soon after that I would like to try again; I even got a library card. I would read books at my office desk (whilst not working!) using my Ugreen tablet stand. However, what became apparent was that I would be tied to the Amazon ecosystem, and if you have read my other work here, you would know I don't like this. I went looking for something smaller that was less tied down. By chance, when I was browsing the CeX website, I came across a Kobo Touch for £30. To me, £30 is take-a-chance kind of money. What could I lose if it doesn't suit me? What? Send it back to CeX so it could find a more suitable home! I'll chance it.

    I read up on the device before I purchased it, of course. No internal light? That's fine. I don't really plan to read in the dark, but I also wasn't sure if it would put me off or if it would be a strain on my eyes. I liked that other features were limited. Sure, I would be able to buy books from the Kobo store through the device, but could I easily add books from other storefronts? As much as I wanted to read, I didn't want to be tied down to one ecosystem again. I was at least relieved about the Wi-Fi because I remember always having to link my iPod Classic up to the PC with its USB cable any time I was downloading podcasts or music. My biggest concern, though, was the battery. I was getting this second-, third-, maybe even fourth-hand, thirteen years after it was released. Were the previous owners careful custodians? Had it been bashed about beyond recognition? Was the battery a shadow of its former self? Most of all, would it be a useful device? There was always the thought I'd buy it and then just forget about it... I mean, what's £30, right? It does its thing and, meh, soon to be forgotten.

    When the device arrived, I still wasn't altogether sure what to expect, as the display is 6 inches, so more like a book but smaller than the Amazon tablet I was using before. I bought the device without a cover. When I held it in my hands, it felt small and light. It wasn't overbearing at all. As much as I love to read, I am also put off by physical books – the idea of seeing a 400+ page book. This big lump of a thing. It feels difficult to physically break down. When I switched it on, the menu was easy to follow. Buying an ebook through the Kobo storefront was simple, albeit I've come to prefer doing it on my phone as it's more intuitive. The initial reading experience was good; it was literally like reading text from a piece of paper. I wasn't sure how I would feel without a backlight.

    One thing I wanted to be able to do with this device, and where it delivers, is that it gives me the ability to read with intention. The experience is just like reading text from a piece of paper; having no backlight makes me focus better. However, where it excels is its customisation. You can change it to suit your needs, whether that's font size or style, or maybe how the pages are presented to you. Rather than being able to see page 87 of 544, I chose to only show the number of pages in each chapter. Seeing that I have 38 pages to read until I've finished a chapter feels like I've gamified reading, especially as I like having the percentage bar at the bottom of the page to show how much of the book I have read overall. Definitely more carrot than stick! I am reading the book for the joy of the story, but I'm also winning (in my head) because I have clear goals per chapter and am passing certain percentages of the book read. It also means that on the days I haven't read as much – some days feel like a slog – I get to have a small victory, which is important. I think without these, I wouldn't use the device.

    The menu layout on the device is easy to follow; there aren't many options. I do feel it can be clumsy to move about and would have preferred tactile buttons, especially for turning pages. It is so easy to press too hard and turn over a couple of pages at a time. There are updates, but they are fairly infrequent. You are basically straight back to where you left off. Because there is no light, you generally don't stay up longer than you need to when reading at night, with the added bonus that it makes you tired and want to fall asleep. How many of us have fallen foul of looking at our phone screens at night, realising it's far too late and now we can't sleep? Plus, I bought a 32 GB SD card for my e-reader, so I literally have a whole library in one small space.

    In terms of getting books onto your SD card or device, you can just download from the Kobo storefront. Remarkably, the device uses a multitude of file formats and doesn't lock out ebooks from other spaces. I quickly found Calibre. It's essentially an iStore for books; the application helps sort all your books and also comes with a function to search for them in different storefronts. The device itself is rather agnostic; as long as it's a file format it uses, it will accept your book. It isn't locked to just accepting DRM-only files; you can upload DRM-free files.

    One thing I had no idea about is the wide variety of ebooks that are available in the public domain. Sure, I understood Dracula was no longer under copyright, but there are services like Project Gutenberg and Standard Ebooks where you can download absolute classics like Moby Dick, The Importance of Being Earnest or even True Story by Lucian of Samosata. That's even more of a reason to pick up an e-reader for me; you already have access to so many great literary tales. A number of modern publishers deliberately don't use DRM on their work. I like this, as it makes it easier to have your books on different devices. You don't need Adobe Digital Editions on everything to unlock them. It's not that I am against DRM; it's fine. I get why it's there, and I certainly have no intention of breaking it. It's just nice when a publisher actually trusts their fans and lets them use the ebook as they wish.

    For me, the journey with my Kobo Touch is more about the books I've read than the device itself, and honestly, I think that's a testament to the product. I'm not writing a hit piece about bad technology, nor am I particularly espousing its bells and whistles. When I've been talking about the e-reader in this blog post, I've been talking about basic functionality and how I have made it work for me. In a sense, the best thing about the device is that it doesn't get in the way. I can be on the edge of my seat, feeling every breath Kyr takes as she fights for her life in Some Desperate Glory, or laugh out loud as Dirk Gently uses the fundamental interconnectedness of all things in Dirk Gently's Holistic Detective Agency. Yes, the device is old, but the battery is still on point. I went to charge it the other day; it hadn't been charged in ages. It was still only at 80%. The software may end up becoming bloated at some point, but I think that's part of its charm. They solved a problem more than 15 years ago with the e-readers that were released, and honestly, I don't think it's been topped yet. There are others out there. Some of the latest models from Kobo themselves just look like the same device, but a bit slimmer and with an add-on to Libby to check out books at your local library. You can get e-readers with a stylus, even a clicker to turn pages remotely.

    I recently looked at e-readers this year. I saw things like the E Ink Android devices from Bigme. Sure, you can access more apps to read your ebooks; that's positive, but it's still a 7-inch tablet, and the battery life won't be as good. I even saw the new miniature Chinese e-readers by companies like Xteink. You need to install newer software on them. It seems cool if you're on the go, but it's certainly not a use case for me. I've not been moved to ditch my Kobo Touch. If you're in the market for an e-reader, it's telling that they are trying to add new features and reinvent the wheel. Find a new way to get people to part with their money. I would absolutely recommend getting an older model with fewer features, as it gives you the power to read with intention. It would also be a brilliant tool to help a child read, as there are no distractions and it can be tailored to their needs. It's like they say: one man's trash is another man's treasure. You will be giving the device a nice new loving home. I think we can call that £30 well spent, can't we!
